## Summary

This article traces a continuous line from 18th-century "electrifications" to today's cold-plasma agronomy. The pivot is metrology: where Bertholon engineered pathways for an indivisible "electric fluid" without access to dose or agents, contemporary plasma agriculture defines sources, quantifies interfacial transfers and measures dose under controlled conditions.

# 1. Electricity and agronomy: a historical trajectory from Antiquity to the 21st century

## 1.1. Electricity (Antiquity–18th century): from experimental spectacle to metrological instrumentation

From Antiquity to the end of the 18th century, the study of electrical phenomena evolved from a qualitative curiosity to a quantitative, instrument-based practice. Early authors such as Theophrastus (*De lapidibus*) and Pliny the Elder (*Natural History*) noted the attraction of straw and other light objects by rubbed amber (*elektron*) and jet (gagates), while medical authors (e.g., Scribonius Largus, Galen) reported the analgesic use of electric fish (torpedoes) **[1–3]**. Medieval lapidaries and encyclopedias widely transmitted these observations in descriptive form, with Albertus Magnus' *De mineralibus* being the emblematic example **[4]**. During the Renaissance, Girolamo Cardano (*De subtilitate*, 1550) and Giambattista della Porta (*Magia naturalis*, 1558/1589) expanded the inventory of "electric bodies" (amber, jet, diamond, sapphire) capable of attracting small particles after friction **[5, 6]**.

A conceptual consolidation began with William Gilbert's work *De Magnete* (1600), who coined the term "electricus" and clearly distinguished electrical attractions from magnetism **[7]**. In the 17th and early 18th centuries, friction machines multiplied demonstrations: Otto von Guericke's sulfur globe (1660s) and Francis Hauksbee's luminous glass globes (1705–1710) are canonical examples **[8, 9]**. Stephen Gray's experiments (1729) established conduction and insulation as distinct behaviors and Charles-François de Cisternay du Fay (1733) organized the effects under a "vitreous/resinous" duality **[10, 11]**.

It was only in the 1740s that advances in instrumentation created conditions for cumulative experiments. The Leyden jar, developed independently by E.-G. von Kleist and P. van Musschenbroek (1745–1746), was the first reliable device for storing electrical charge (Figure 1a). Linking several jars in parallel to form a "battery" further increased storage capacity (Figure 1b) **[12–14]**. At the same time, electrostatic machines became more standardized, exemplified by Nairne's cylinder (1773; Figure 1c) and van Marum's monumental generator (1784) **[15, 16]**. Public demonstrations provided a bridge between spectacle and experiment: on March 14, 1746, Abbé Nollet famously discharged a Leyden jar through a chain of the king's guards at Versailles and then through Carthusian monks **[17, 18]**.

Metrological tools multiplied: Franklin's demonstration of the equivalence between lightning and electricity and his promotion of the lightning rod (1750–1752) **[17, 21, 22]**. Lane's spark-reading electrometer (1767) allowed the quantification of Leyden jar discharge, while the Henley quadrant (1770) introduced a single-index measure of repulsion **[23–25]**. H.-B. de Saussure extended electrometers to atmospheric studies during the 1760s–1780s **[26]**. This quantitative turn culminated with Coulomb's torsion balance (1785), which yielded the first precise law of electrical forces **[27]**. Synthesis works such as Priestley's *History and Present State of Electricity* (1767/1769), Cavallo's *Complete Treatise of Electricity* (1777) and Beccaria's *Dell'elettricismo artificiale e naturale* (1753) organized the expanding literature **[14, 28–30]**.

Having established tools for storage, delivery and measurement, the first applications to living systems began to emerge. In 1783, Bertholon's *De l'électricité des végétaux* introduced the "electro-végétomètre," a device designed to capture and channel atmospheric electricity toward crop beds **[31]**. Stephen Demainbray in the 1740s electrified myrtles and observed accelerated growth, results confirmed by Abbé Nollet on mustard seeds and Jean Jallabert on hyacinth and daffodil bulbs. Later, Edouard-François Nunebert compared non-electrified and electrified onion bulbs, noting faster germination and taller stems **[31]**.

## 1.2. 1790–2000: From galvanism to controlled discharges and their processes

Volta's invention of the pile (1799–1800; Figure 2a) marked a decisive turning point — for the first time, experimenters had access to a continuous, controllable and reproducible source of current **[32]**. Through the 19th century, this foundation was reinforced by the standardization of instruments and units: precision galvanometers and bridges, standard cells and the practical volt–ohm–ampere system together provided a calibrated metrological framework.

Faraday formalized the laws of electrolysis in the 1830s **[36]**. In biology, Burdon-Sanderson recorded action potentials in *Dionaea muscipula* (1873), showing that plant movements could be associated with discrete electrical signals **[37]**. A generation later, Bose extended this program into plant electrophysiology, developing instruments and methods to detect, transmit and analyze plant signals (Figure 2b) **[33]**.

> ![](./images/image2.jpeg)

> ***Figure 2. (a) Voltaic pile (1800): column of alternating zinc-copper disks separated by brine-soaked washers [20]. (b) Bose's apparatus for simultaneous mechanical and electrical responses [33]. (c) Crookes tube (high vacuum) demonstrating cathode rays [34]. (d) Werner von Siemens's first ozonizer (1857) [35].***

From the second half of the 19th century, a true metrology of discharges began to emerge. Paschen formulated his law (1889) relating breakdown voltage to pressure and electrode spacing **[38]**. Townsend's ionization theory (1897–1901) clarified the avalanche multiplication of electrons **[39, 40]**. Geissler's tubes and Crookes's rarefied-gas experiments (Figure 2c) revealed the diversity of luminous phenomena under reduced pressure **[34, 41]**. As early as 1857, Werner von Siemens described the dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) as a practical source for ozone generation (Figure 2d) **[35]**. By 1928, Langmuir introduced the term plasma to describe a quasi-neutral ionized gas **[42]**.

On this metrological foundation, structured chemical applications followed. Siemens's DBD ozonizers enabled large-scale water ozonation **[43, 44]**. The Birkeland–Eyde arc (1903, Figure 3a) harnessed high-temperature plasmas to oxidize atmospheric nitrogen, producing nitrates as precursors for fertilizers **[45]**. Low-pressure mercury-vapor lamps systematized the germicidal use of ultraviolet radiation (Figure 3b) **[46–48]**.

> ![](./images/image3.jpeg)

> ***Figure 3. (a) Birkeland–Eyde disc flame [45]. (b) UV-germicidal aerosol test duct [48].***

At the beginning of the 20th century, electroculture experienced renewed interest. Devices ranged from Lemström's overhead networks of charged wires to Christofleau's "atmospheric" antennas **[49, 50]**. As shown in Figure 4a, the latter were designed to channel "natural electricity" from diverse sources into the soil. Figure 4b depicts their application to grapevines, while Figure 4c illustrates reports of enhanced bean growth. Yet these outcomes must be interpreted with caution: fertilization regimes, soil heterogeneity, seasonal effects and device geometries were poorly controlled.

> ![](./images/image4.jpeg)

> ***Figure 4. (a) Schematic of Christofleau's atmospheric antenna [50]. (b) Application to vines [50]. (c) Beans grown with manure (left) vs. under Christofleau's antenna (right) [50].***

This historical current of electroculture did not directly evolve into plasma-based technologies. The latter emerged independently at the end of the 20th century, driven by advances in plasma physics. From the 1970s onward, cold plasmas began to be investigated as low-temperature agents for sterilization. By the 1990s, hydrogen peroxide gas-plasma sterilizers entered healthcare practice **[51, 52]**. In the mid-to-late 1990s, atmospheric sources (DBDs, glidarc reactors, plasma jets) that operated directly in open air emerged **[53]**.

## 1.3. The 21st century: the rise of plasma agriculture

Plasma agriculture designates a contemporary research field that applies cold plasma sources to seeds, plants, soils, irrigation systems and agri-food chains. Bibliometric analysis (Figure 5a) shows that the term "plasma agriculture" gained traction only after the mid-2010s.

Cold plasmas are non-thermal discharges whose efficacy arises from the co-action of five properties (Figure 5b):

- **Electrical:** transient fields, ionization fronts and pulsed currents
- **Chemical:** reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) — short-lived (OH, O, NO) and long-lived (H₂O₂, O₃, NO₂⁻/NO₃⁻)
- **Radiative:** UV and VUV photons that couple to DNA/protein targets
- **Thermal:** bulk gas remains near ambient, though local heating (10–50 °C) can be engineered
- **Fluid-mechanical:** ionic wind and microjets enhance mass transfer at plasma–liquid boundaries

> ![](./images/image5.png)

> ***Figure 5. (a) Annual publications containing "plasma agriculture" (Google Scholar, 1990–2024). (b) Schematic of seeds treated by CAP with its 5 coupled properties.***

By 2025, plasma agriculture is organized around four application axes:

- **Seed treatment:** plasma-induced surface modification and low-dose RONS/UV to accelerate germination **[57, 58]**. PAW chemistry is routinely quantified by dose tracers **[59]**.
- **Plant treatment:** CAP, afterglows and PAW under strict thermal control to elicit signaling rather than damage **[60–62]**.
- **Media treatment:** irrigation water, soil and air — CAP and PAW enable gentle disinfection and modulation of microbial communities **[63, 64]**.
- **Agri-food treatment:** CAP and PAW to sanitize produce, packaging and equipment **[65–67]**.

In contemporary plasma agriculture, progress is defined by a physics-based framework of explicit dosimetry: quantitative, traceable dose metrics (RONS concentrations, UV fluence, electrical dose, exposure time) are measured at the target.

# 2. Reappraising Abbé Bertholon's contribution to agricultural electricity (1780s)

## 2.1. A brief scientific biography

Pierre-Nicolas Bertholon de Saint-Lazare (1741–1800), a priest, physicist and Enlightenment pedagogue, built his career in Montpellier, where he taught experimental physics **[68]**. During the 1780s he emerged as a central figure of experimental electricity, notable for his focus on applications rather than spectacle. He combined public instruction with carefully staged demonstrations, framing his program around meteorological electricity, animal electricity and plant electricity **[69]**.

Bertholon designed the electro-végétomètre, promoted "medical electricity" and advocated for lightning rods in Languedoc. He participated in the early aerostatic movement, launching a hot-air balloon with Jean-Antoine Chaptal in 1783 **[69, 71]**.

## 2.2. Bertholon's thesis: electricity as a stimulus to vegetation

Bertholon advanced a striking thesis: that atmospheric electricity was a constant reservoir capable of stimulating the entire physiology of plants — germination, sap circulation, nutrition, secretion, growth, flowering, fruiting and transpiration **[31]**. His treatise was structured in three parts:

1. Demonstrate the existence and influence of atmospheric electricity
2. Catalogue its effects on plant functions
3. Operationalize these effects through dedicated instruments, most notably the electro-végétomètre

He assembled three types of evidence: qualitative correlations (storms coinciding with faster germination), instrumented observations (electrometers, kites and electroscopes) and mechanistic conjectures (electricity driving sap circulation through "electrical affinity").

The strength of Bertholon's work lies in its unifying vision. The limits were equally clear: no dosimetry was attempted, the roles of different agents were not distinguished and much reasoning remained analogical.

## 2.4. Technical and methodological limitations

By the late 18th century, Bertholon's program was limited above all by instrumentation. Lane's spark electrometer (1767) and Henley's quadrant (c. 1770) provided only coarse, qualitative indicators **[72, 73]**. Coulomb's torsion balance (1785), though it established the first quantitative law of electrostatics, post-dated Bertholon's writings and measured forces rather than energy deposition **[74, 75]**.

The conceptual framework was equally incomplete. Ozone was not recognized as a distinct chemical species until 1840 **[76]**, while ultraviolet radiation was discovered only in 1801 **[77, 78]**. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) and electrohydrodynamic flows were entirely unknown. In *De l'Électricité des Végétaux* (pp. 399, 401), Bertholon himself reported luminous aigrettes visible at night on the multi-point terminals — modern physics recognizes these as corona discharges: localized plasmas that release ions, electrons, ozone and nitrogen oxides **[31, 79]**.

Experimental practice fell well short of modern standards. Bertholon relied on qualitative accounts with no systematic replication, randomization or formal control groups. The methodological tools required to stabilize inference would only appear in the 20th century **[80, 81]**.

In contrast, modern plasma agriculture defines "dose" through explicit, quantitative parameters: electric field strength (V·m⁻¹·s), surface charge density (C·m⁻²) and specific energy deposition (J·kg⁻¹ or J·m⁻²) **[54, 56, 82]**.

# 3. Modern physics of cold plasmas applied to agriculture

## 3.1. Definition of cold plasma & key properties

Plasma is the fourth state of matter, characterized by macroscopic quasi-neutrality (approximately balanced positive-ion and electron densities) and collective dynamics dominated by long-range Coulomb interactions. Two fundamental parameters govern its electrostatic behavior:

- **Debye length** λ_D = √(ε₀ k_B T_e / n_e e²)
- **Electron plasma frequency** ω_p = √(n_e e² / ε₀ m_e)

A medium qualifies as plasma when λ_D ≪ L (system size), N_D = (4/3)πn_e λ_D³ ≫ 1, and collective effects dominate over binary collisions **[83, 84]**.

A **cold atmospheric plasma (CAP)** is a non-equilibrium discharge in which the electron temperature T_e (≈1–5 eV, i.e., 11,600–58,000 K) greatly exceeds the gas temperature T_g (≈300–350 K). At atmospheric pressure, one observes streamer regimes (channeled microdischarges <10 ns) and dielectric-barrier discharges (DBDs), which may be filamentary or diffuse **[84]**.

CAPs couple multiple processes: the electric field drives charge transport while RONS include short-lived species (O(³P), OH•, ¹O₂, NO•) and long-lived species (O₃, H₂O₂, NO₂⁻/NO₃⁻) **[87, 88]**. The energy chain: source → electrons → elementary reactions → reactive species → interfacial fluxes **[89]**.

Common atmospheric sources include **DBDs** (planar, coaxial) driven sinusoidally or in pulsed mode (kHz–MHz) **[90]** and **atmospheric pressure plasma jets (APPJ)** operating in helium or argon **[91]**.

Metrological qualification includes: electrical diagnostics (V(t), I(t), Lissajous figures), ICCD imaging for streamer visualization, gas temperature from rotational spectroscopy, and physico-chemical analysis (H₂O₂, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻, pH, conductivity) **[87, 92–96]**.

## 3.2. Technologies and laboratory protocols

CAP is implemented through three complementary routes:

- **DDS (Direct Dry-Surface exposure):** plasma applied directly to tissues with no intentional free water
- **DWS (Direct Wet-Surface exposure):** plasma applied with a thin aqueous film at the interface
- **PAW (Plasma-Activated Water):** water treated ex situ by plasma, then applied via soaking, spraying, irrigation or postharvest rinsing **[58]**

Indicative operating ranges:
- **DBD:** 6–20 kVpp, 5–50 kHz, gap 0.5–5 mm, exposure 0.5–60 s
- **APPJ:** 2–10 kVpp, 10–200 kHz or RF, 0.5–5 L·min⁻¹ He/Ar, nozzle-to-tissue 5–30 mm

For direct exposures, dose is expressed as specific energy (J·m⁻² for leaves, J·kg⁻¹ for seed lots), electrical operating conditions, gas composition/flow, source-target distance, scan speed, exposure time and effluent temperature **[98, 99]**.

For PAW, characterization includes specific electrical energy (J·L⁻¹), chemical state (H₂O₂, NO₂⁻/NO₃⁻, pH, conductivity) and dose applied **[87, 100, 101]**.

## 3.3. Effects of cold plasma on seeds

CAP exerts three major, interdependent influences on seeds: (1) alteration of seed coat physico-chemistry, (2) reduction of microbial load and (3) triggering of early physiological and molecular responses.

### 3.3.1. Seed-coat modification and wettability

Microscopy studies (AFM/SEM) consistently show that CAP alters the outer surface of seeds by roughening and restructuring the coat through etching, ablation and nano/micro-structuring. In wheat and barley, Ar–O₂ plasma treatments produced visibly fissured, rougher surfaces that correlated with increased water uptake **[102, 103]**.

> ![](./images/image9.jpeg)

> ***Figure 9. SEM images of wheat seed surfaces treated for 90 s with (a) control (no plasma), (b) Ar/O₂ plasma. Scale bar is 50 μm. Reproduced from Rahman et al., Scientific Reports, 2018, CC BY 4.0 [102].***

Surface chemistry is also transformed: plasma introduces oxygen-containing functional groups, increasing surface energy **[107, 108]**. One of the clearest signatures is the dramatic reduction in water contact angle (WCA). In lentil, bean and wheat, RF air plasma reduced WCA from 127°, 98° and 115° to 20°, 53° and nearly 0°, respectively (Figure 10) **[109]**.

### 3.3.2. Decontamination and pathogen control

CAP treatments achieve substantial reductions in surface microbial loads for maize, wheat, sunflower and other seeds **[111]**. Figure 11a demonstrates effective fungal decontamination of sunflower seeds following a 5-min plasma treatment. In chickpea, 2–5 minutes of CAP lowered natural microbiota by 1–2 log units **[112]**. On hazelnuts, low-pressure SF₆ plasma achieved up to a 5-log reduction of *Aspergillus parasiticus* **[113]**.

> ![](./images/image11.jpeg)

> ***Figure 11. (a) Sunflower seeds contaminated by Rhizopus: untreated vs. plasma-treated for 5 min (T. Dufour, LPP). (b) Effect of CAP on germination of Cicer arietinum and survival of micro-organisms [112]. (c) Reduction of A. parasiticus on hazelnuts [113].***

However, complete microbial inactivation remains uncommon and species-dependent. In sweet basil, DBD treatments reduced *Fusarium oxysporum* but viable colonies persisted within seed recesses **[114]**.

### 3.3.3. Physiological and molecular responses

Plasma-generated RONS promote abscisic acid (ABA) catabolism, enhance gibberellic acid (GA) signaling, interact with nitric oxide (NO) and induce expression of cell wall-weakening genes **[116, 117]**.

At the physiological level, one of the most consistent outcomes is up-regulation of antioxidant defenses. In oilseed rape exposed to drought, CAP improved germination and enhanced SOD and CAT activities while reducing lipid peroxidation (MDA) (Figure 12) **[118]**.

> ![](./images/image12.jpeg)

> ***Figure 12. Effect of drought stress and cold plasma treatment on 4 biomarkers in oilseed rape seedlings: (a) SOD activity, (b) CAT activity, (c) MDA contents, (d) soluble sugar contents. WW: well watered; DS: drought stress. Adapted from [118].***

In rice under cold stress, argon DBD accelerated germination and triggered transcriptomic shifts including suppression of NCED3 and induction of stress-tolerance genes **[119]**. Similar trends were reported in soybean **[120]**.

## 3.4. Effects of cold plasma on seedlings and plants

CAP can stimulate growth and stress tolerance in multiple species, though outcomes remain strongly dose- and context-dependent. PAW promoted tomato seedling growth and nutritional quality, while basil exposed to DBD showed elevated chlorophylls and antioxidant activity **[123, 124]**. Long-term benefits of PAW irrigation are visible in tomato and pepper, where seedlings remained larger and more vigorous even 60 days after sowing (Figures 13a–b) **[125]**.

In tomato, moderate PAW treatments (15–30 min) maximized seedling development at 28 days, while longer exposures became less effective (Figure 13c) **[123]**. Lentil seedlings irrigated with plasma-activated water displayed stronger growth compared with controls at 18 days (Figure 13d) **[126]**.

> ![](./images/image13.jpeg)

> ***Figure 13. (a–b) Long-term effect of PAW on tomato (a) and pepper (b) seedlings, 60th day after sowing [125]. (c) Effect of PAW exposure time on tomato seedling growth (28 days old) [123]. (d) Lentil seedlings irrigated with tap water (TW), plasma-activated tap water (PATW), demineralized water (DM) or plasma-activated demineralized water (PADM), 18th day [126].***

Plasma also affects photosynthetic performance (PSII). Low doses improve photochemical parameters, while higher doses inhibit them **[127, 128]**. CAP/PAW often upregulates antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, POX) and modulates secondary metabolism **[124, 129]**. Tomato seedlings sprayed with PAW showed up to 61% reduced disease severity against *Xanthomonas* **[130]**.

The redox-active chemistry of PAW has been linked to improved tolerance to salinity and drought **[128, 132]**. At the root level, maize showed accelerated endodermal lignification under arsenic exposure, suggesting plasma-induced anatomical "fortification" **[133]**.

# 4. Conclusion & outlooks

This article has traced a continuous, if discontinuous, line from 18th-century "electrifications" to today's cold-plasma agronomy. The pivot is metrology. Where Bertholon engineered pathways for an indivisible "electric fluid" without access to dose or agents, contemporary plasma agriculture defines sources, quantifies interfacial transfers and measures dose under controlled conditions.

Reappraised in this light, Bertholon's contribution is methodological rather than evidentiary: he supplied an operative vocabulary and the intuition that moderate electrical regimes might beneficially modulate plant functions. Modern work explains when and why that intuition holds.

The rupture with historical electroculture is threefold: the nature of the electrical object (from a unitary essence to a multi-physics process), the means of measurement and control (from qualitative signs to chemical/energetic dosimetry) and the standards of validation (from compelling trials to randomized, replicated designs).

Looking ahead, four priorities define an applied agenda:

1. **Anchor practice in traceable dosing** for both CAP and PAW routes
2. **Scale up** with eco-designed, air-fed sources and low-energy PAW reactors
3. **Integrate plasma processes** within agroecological itineraries
4. **Ensure safe and robust adoption** through ozone/NOₓ and UV limits, arc protection and multicenter trials

Seen across three centuries, the motif of an "électricité vivifiante" endures, but its status is transformed. It is no longer a regulative idea grounded in atmospheric reservoirs; it is a mechanistic toolbox capable of delivering lower-input, traceable and potentially more resilient crop systems.
